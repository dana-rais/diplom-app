# 03. Literature Review
### Research topic: Development of a Mobile Application for Teaching Digital Literacy to Older Adults with Interactive Training Simulations

## 3.1. Introduction

Smartphones and online services are now used for communication, banking, healthcare, information searching, and access to public services. Older adults may find these technologies difficult because of limited prior experience, unfamiliar interfaces, accessibility barriers, and concerns about online security. Effective and accessible digital literacy education for this group is therefore both a research and a software development challenge.

This review critically examines studies published between 2021 and 2025 in three areas: digital literacy training programmes for older adults, instructional strategies and interactive learning, and usability and accessibility of mobile applications. One foundational study (Czaja et al., 2006) is retained to explain why older adults’ technology adoption depends on more than access. The review addresses three questions: which approaches already exist, what is known about their effectiveness, and which limitations the proposed project must take into account.

## 3.2. Digital Literacy Training Programmes for Older Adults
Several recent studies show that structured training can improve older adults’ digital skills. Seaton et al. (2023) evaluated the Gluu Essentials programme for middle-aged and older adults in a pre-post design and reported improved mobile device skills and wider use of online activities. A notable limitation is that not all participants completed the follow-up survey, which weakens generalisation.

Quialheiro et al. (2023) studied the OITO project, an in-person programme of eight workshops that used mobile devices to improve digital proficiency and health literacy. Siriwattanarat et al. (2025) evaluated a structured workshop for adults aged 60 and over in Bangkok (84 participants) using the Mobile Device Proficiency Questionnaire. They found that participants aged 70 and over with lower education had comparatively lower digital literacy. Both programmes depend on scheduled sessions and instructors, which limits independent practice.

Wilson-Menzfeld et al. (2023) examined one-to-one online digital skills training and showed the value of individualised support. Miller et al. (2024) used a single-group pretest-posttest design to evaluate digital literacy training for low-income older adults delivered through undergraduate community-engaged learning. The lack of a control group limits causal interpretation, but the study shows that financial and social circumstances must be considered alongside technical skills.

Overall, these programmes can improve practical skills, but most rely on human instructors, fixed sessions, or community organisation. They do not show whether a standalone mobile application could provide comparable practice opportunities.

## 3.3. Instructional Strategies and Interactive Learning

Schirmer et al. (2022) argued, on the basis of interviews with instructors, that training should be connected to older people’s “lifeworld”: their daily routines, needs, and motivations. This supports organising learning content around real tasks, such as messaging family members, booking appointments, or recognising a suspicious message. The study, however, offers a conceptual explanation and does not test a mobile learning tool.

Ahmad, N. A. et al. (2022) conducted a systematic literature review of instructional strategies for older adults learning digital technologies. Unlike earlier reviews focused on one technology, it covers digital technologies in general and stresses that instruction should be adapted to older learners. The authors also note that evidence on the effectiveness of specific strategies is still limited. Ali et al. (2025) reviewed 14 studies on intergenerational methods (such as collaborative learning, reverse mentoring, and family-based learning) and found that these methods can support digital application use. They also depend on the availability of another person.

Moore and Hancock (2022) showed that an interactive online media literacy programme improved older adults’ resilience to fake news. Its exercises required learners to apply knowledge rather than only read information. The intervention, however, targeted misinformation, not general smartphone skills, and answering questions is not the same as practising a sequence of actions in a realistic task. A simulation that asks the learner to inspect a message, identify warning signs, and choose a response trains procedural skill. This advantage remains a research hypothesis that must be tested with the target users.

## 3.4. Usability and Accessibility of Mobile Applications

Gomez-Hernandez et al. (2023) carried out a systematic review and thematic analysis of design guidelines for mobile apps for older adults. Their recommendations concern navigation, visual design, interaction, cognitive load, and help and training. Ahmad, B. et al. (2021) provide a complementary empirical set of usability recommendations for smartphone applications for older adults. Both inform decisions about readable text, large and well-spaced controls, consistent navigation, and step-by-step guidance. Guidelines alone, however, do not guarantee usability for a particular app and user group.

Wang et al. (2022) reviewed methods for evaluating the usability of mobile health applications for older adults. Their findings show that evaluation should combine several methods, for example questionnaires, interviews, observation, and task performance, rather than relying on satisfaction ratings alone. Because the review concerns health apps, its conclusions cannot be transferred directly to educational simulations, and evaluation measures for this project must be chosen specifically for learning digital skills.

Czaja et al. (2006), a foundational study, showed that technology use among older adults is predicted by attitudes, abilities, and confidence, not just access. This supports combining accessible design with confidence-building practice.

## 3.5. Existing Approaches and Their Limitations

The literature identifies workshops (Quialheiro et al., 2023; Siriwattanarat et al., 2025), one-to-one instruction (Wilson-Menzfeld et al., 2023), community-engaged programmes (Miller et al., 2024), interactive online modules (Moore & Hancock, 2022), and intergenerational methods (Ali et al., 2025). Three recurring limitations emerge:
  - **Dependence on human support. Many programmes need instructors, volunteers, or scheduled sessions, which restricts independent repetition.
  - **Fragmented content. Programmes concentrate on separate topics (misinformation, general device proficiency, specific services), and usability guidelines address interface design rather than learning effectiveness.
  - **Limited evidence. Studies often have small or non-randomised samples, no control groups (Miller et al., 2024), or incomplete follow-up (Seaton et al., 2023), and evidence on individual instructional strategies remains limited (Ahmad, N. A. et al., 2022).

## 3.6. Research Gap

The reviewed studies show that digital literacy training can improve older adults’ skills and that accessible design principles are well documented. Yet these two lines of research are rarely combined, and few studies evaluate a mobile application that gives learners repeatable, simulated practice of everyday digital tasks. The gap addressed by this project is the design and evaluation of a mobile learning application that combines:

  - **an accessible interface based on published usability guidelines;
  - **interactive training simulations of realistic tasks (using a messaging app, searching for information, recognising suspicious messages and fraud attempts);
  - **immediate feedback and assessment, with repeatable exercises and pre- and post-training measurement.

The novelty lies not in claiming that educational apps do not exist, but in this integration and in its empirical evaluation with older adults.

The proposed application will combine them, so its evaluation should measure usability, task completion in simulations, and retention of skills.

## 3.8. Conclusion

Studies from 2021–2025 indicate that older adults benefit from structured digital literacy education, especially when content is relevant to daily life and adapted to learners’ needs. Interactive exercises encourage the application of knowledge, and accessible design reduces avoidable difficulties. The studies also have limitations in sample size, retention, research design, and transferability across application domains, so the proposed application must be evaluated directly with older adults through usability testing, task-completion rates, pre- and post-training assessments, and, where feasible, follow-up testing.
